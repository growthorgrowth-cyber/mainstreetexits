Most owners start thinking about selling when they are tired. That is the worst time to start preparing. A buyer is paying for the next several years of profit, and they will look hard for reasons to pay less. The good news: most of those reasons can be fixed if you give yourself a runway.

Here is how we would use one to three years.

First: get a baseline

Before you change anything, find out what the business would likely sell for today and what is holding the number down. You cannot prioritize without that. A baseline valuation should show your recast earnings (SDE or EBITDA), a realistic multiple range, and a short list of the issues a buyer would flag.

Year 1: clean up the numbers and reduce your role

Make the books buyer-ready

  • Get your profit and loss statements to match your tax returns.
  • Stop running personal expenses through the business, or at least track every one so it can be added back with proof.
  • Record all revenue. Unreported cash cannot be counted toward value, and a lender will not finance it.
  • Close the books monthly so you can show trailing twelve months at any time.

Start pulling yourself out of the day to day

Write down what only you do. Then start handing those jobs off: quoting, scheduling, key customer relationships, ordering, hiring. Promote or hire someone who can run the floor. A buyer is far more comfortable with a business that already runs without its owner, and so is their lender.

Year 2: strengthen what buyers pay a premium for

  • Spread out your customers. If one customer is a big slice of revenue, work on growing others. Put key customers under contract where it makes sense.
  • Build recurring revenue. Service agreements, maintenance plans and memberships are worth more to a buyer than one-off jobs.
  • Lock in your lease. A buyer's lender will want the lease to run long enough to match the loan. Talk to your landlord about term, renewal options and assignment before you list.
  • Keep your key people. Fair pay, clear roles and, where appropriate, retention incentives tied to a sale.
  • Document processes. Simple checklists and how-to guides are enough. They show a buyer the business is teachable.

Year 3 (or the last 6 to 12 months): get market-ready

  • Keep revenue and margins steady or rising. A dip right before a sale is expensive.
  • Clean up legal loose ends: expired licenses, liens, open disputes, missing contracts.
  • Gather three years of returns and statements, your lease, contracts and an equipment list.
  • Meet with your CPA about deal structure and taxes, and know your net number after taxes and debt.
  • Decide what you want after the sale, including how long you are willing to help the buyer transition.

Why this matters right now

BizBuySell's Q3 2026 Insight Report found buyers are becoming more selective, gravitating toward businesses with reliable cash flow, clean financials, limited owner dependence and a clear path to financing (source). The businesses that check those boxes get the attention. The ones that do not tend to sit.

The short version

  1. Get a baseline valuation.
  2. Clean the books.
  3. Make yourself less necessary.
  4. Strengthen customers, lease, team and processes.
  5. Keep performance strong into the sale.
