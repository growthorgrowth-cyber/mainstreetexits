Selling to an SBA-financed buyer: what owners need to know

By Eric Camehl | October 24, 2026

A large share of main street buyers finance their purchase with an SBA 7(a) loan. That is good news for sellers: it means more buyers can afford your business and you get most of your money at closing. It also means a lender, and the SBA's rules, become part of your deal. Here is what that looks like from the seller's side.

SBA rules change. The points below reflect SBA's SOP 50 10 8.1 as summarized by Womble Bond Dickinson (source). Your buyer's lender will confirm current requirements for your specific deal.

The lender orders an independent valuation

For a change of ownership, the lender gets an independent business valuation from a qualified provider. If the price is higher than that valuation supports, the gap generally has to be covered by more buyer equity, not more debt. In plain terms: an inflated asking price is a problem you will run into in underwriting, even after you have a signed deal. Pricing from real earnings up front avoids it.

The buyer needs real cash in the deal

For a buyer acquiring a business, SBA requires an equity injection of at least 10% of total project costs. Some of that can come from limited sources, including a seller note, but those limited sources can make up no more than half of the required injection. The rest has to come from sources like the buyer's own cash.

Seller notes may have to sit on standby

If your seller note is being counted toward the buyer's equity injection, it has to be subordinated to the lender and on full standby, meaning no payments of principal or interest during the term of the SBA loan. That is a long time to wait. A note that is not counted as equity can be structured differently, subject to the lender's terms. Know which kind you are agreeing to before you sign a letter of intent.

Your transition period has limits

In a full sale, the seller generally has to exit ownership, but can stay on as a consultant to help the buyer transition. Under SOP 50 10 8.1 that consulting period can run up to 24 months. Your actual transition terms are negotiated in the deal.

Financials get a hard look

The lender will underwrite from your tax returns and verify that the business's cash flow can cover the loan payments. Clean, reconciled financials and well-documented add-backs make this go faster. Messy ones can delay or kill a closing.

If your returns and P&Ls do not match today, fix that first. The three-year preparation checklist explains why lenders care and what to line up.

The timeline includes the lender

Underwriting, the valuation, the lease review and closing documents all take time. The IBBA and M&A Source Market Pulse survey for Q2 2026 found main street deals averaged six to 10 months from engagement to close (source). Having documents ready shortens your piece of it.

How to be ready for an SBA buyer

  • Three years of business tax returns, P&Ls and balance sheets, plus year to date
  • A documented list of add-backs
  • A lease that runs long enough, with assignment terms your landlord will honor
  • Equipment and inventory lists
  • A realistic price the valuation can support
  • A decision on whether you are willing to carry a seller note, and on what terms
