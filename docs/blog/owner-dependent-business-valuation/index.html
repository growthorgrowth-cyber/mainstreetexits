Owner-dependent businesses sell for far less. Here is why, and how to fix it.

By Eric Camehl | October 24, 2026

Some of the hardest conversations I have are with owners who are great at what they do. They built a strong business, they take home good money, and the customers love them. Then we start talking about value, and it turns out the thing that made them successful is the thing holding the price down: the business runs on them.

If the business needs you to make sales, price jobs, solve problems and keep customers happy, a buyer is not buying a company. They are buying a high-paying job with a lot of risk attached. And people do not pay much for a job.

In my experience, an owner-dependent business often sells for something closer to half of what the same earnings would bring in a business that runs without the owner, and sometimes it does not sell at all. That is my experience, not a published statistic. Below I will show you the published data that points the same direction, why the gap exists, and what you can do about it.

A job vs. an asset

Think about it from the buyer's chair. A business is worth something to a buyer because of the profit it will produce after they own it. If that profit depends on one person's relationships, skills, license or 60-hour weeks, and that person is leaving, the buyer has to ask a hard question: how much of this survives without you?

An asset produces income whether or not the owner shows up every day. It has a team, systems, customers who are loyal to the company and processes anyone can follow. A job produces income only as long as someone does the work. You can sell an asset. You can only sell a job to someone willing to step into it, and they will discount the price for taking on the work and the risk.

What the data says about owner dependence and value

Value Builder System: 2.93x vs. 4.49x

The Value Builder System, which scores businesses on eight drivers of value, analyzed about 14,000 business owners who reported receiving an offer to buy their company. The average offer was 3.7 times pre-tax profit. Where the owner did not know customers personally and rarely got involved in serving them, offers averaged 4.49 times. Where the owner knew every customer by first name and customers expected the owner to be personally involved, offers averaged 2.93 times (Value Builder study, as published).

That is roughly a 35% lower multiple, and it measures just one dimension of owner dependence: who holds the customer relationships. In a real business, owner dependence usually shows up in several places at once, in sales, operations, licensing and decision making, and each one adds risk.

Size, SDE and EBITDA

The IBBA and M&A Source Market Pulse survey for Q2 2026 reported average multiples by deal size: 2.0x SDE for businesses under $500,000, 2.8x SDE for $500,000 to $1 million, 3.1x SDE for $1 million to $2 million, 4.0x EBITDA for $2 million to $5 million, and 5.8x EBITDA for $5 million to $50 million (IBBA Q2 2026 highlights).

Be careful comparing those directly. SDE includes one owner's pay and EBITDA generally does not, so the denominators are different, and size drives much of the gap. But the pattern tells you something real. The smaller and more owner-run a business is, the more it is priced as the owner's income, at a lower multiple. As a business grows a management layer and its earnings stand on their own, buyers start valuing it on EBITDA and the multiples climb. Removing yourself from the center of the business is part of how a company moves from one bracket to the next.

What buyers say they want

BizBuySell's president, commenting on its Q3 2026 Insight Report, said buyers are gravitating toward businesses with reliable cash flow, clean financials, limited owner dependence and a clear path to financing (BizBuySell Insight Report; CoStar press release). Limited owner dependence is on that short list for a reason.

Why buyers pay less for an owner-dependent business

Risk: will the revenue walk out the door with you?

If customers buy because of you, some of them may leave when you do. A buyer cannot know how many, so they assume the worst and price it in.

The buyer is buying a job

If the new owner has to work full time to keep the business going, part of the earnings are really just their own salary. A buyer will mentally subtract a fair wage for the work before deciding what the business itself is worth.

Financing gets harder

Lenders care about whether the cash flow continues after the sale. Under SBA's current rules for a full change of ownership, the seller generally has to exit, though they can stay on as a consultant for up to 24 months (Womble Bond Dickinson on SOP 50 10 8.1). If the business only works with you in it, a temporary consulting period may not be enough to convince a lender, or a buyer.

More of the price gets pushed into terms

When buyers are unsure the earnings will hold, they shift risk back to the seller. In my experience that means a larger seller note, an earnout that only pays if results hold up, a holdback, or a longer required transition. The headline price may look fine, but less of it is cash at closing and more of it depends on a business you no longer control.

Fewer buyers

The IBBA's Q2 2026 survey found smaller deals under $500,000 frequently receive just one or two offers, while 87% of deals over $5 million attracted at least three (IBBA Market Pulse Q2 2026). Owner dependence narrows the pool further, to buyers who have the exact skills, license or relationships to step into your shoes. Fewer bidders means less competition on price.

Here is how a buyer thinks about Business A. They pay $600,000 and step into a 55-hour week. If a fair salary for that work is, say, $90,000 (also hypothetical), the business is really earning $210,000 on top of their wages, and only if the customers stay. For Business B, the $300,000 already reflects the cost of a manager. The buyer is paying for profit, not for the privilege of working.

How buyers spot owner dependence

You will not be able to hide it, and you should not try. Experienced buyers find it quickly:

  • The questions. "Who prices jobs? Who do your top customers call? What happens when you take two weeks off?"
  • The website and reviews. Your cell phone is the main number. Reviews mention you by name, not the company.
  • The paperwork. You sign every quote, every check and every contract. The license or key certification is in your personal name.
  • The org chart. There isn't one, or every line points to you.
  • The customer list. Your biggest accounts are personal relationships, sometimes friends.
  • The systems. Pricing, scheduling and vendor deals live in your head or your personal email.
  • The site visit. Employees look to you for every answer while the buyer is in the room.

How to fix it over one to three years

The good news is that owner dependence is one of the most fixable problems in a business, if you give it time. Buyers and lenders want to see the business performing without you, ideally for a full year or more, so start well before you list. For the bigger picture, see Preparing a business for sale: start three years out.

1. Put a manager in place

Hire or promote someone to run daily operations. Give them real authority, not just a title, and let them make decisions you would have made. Yes, it costs money, and it may lower your profit for a while. A business that runs under a manager is the thing buyers pay a higher multiple for.

2. Write your SOPs

Document the work that only you know how to do: how you price, how you sell, how you hire, how you handle a complaint, how you order. Checklists and short videos are fine. The goal is that a capable person could follow them without calling you.

3. Hand off customer relationships

Introduce your key customers to the people who will serve them. Bring a team member to meetings, then send them alone. Move customers off your cell phone and onto a company line and email. Over time the relationship moves from you to the company.

4. Build recurring revenue

Service agreements, maintenance plans, memberships and contracts make revenue more predictable and less tied to your personal selling. Predictable revenue lowers risk in a buyer's eyes.

5. Document systems

Move customer data, quoting, scheduling and job history into a CRM or field service system the business owns. Make sure vendor accounts, software logins and domain names belong to the company.

6. Deal with licenses

If the business operates on your personal license, figure out whether an employee can obtain the qualifying license, or what a buyer would need. Some licenses take months or years to get.

7. Test it

Take a real vacation. Start with a few days and work up to two weeks. Whatever breaks while you are gone is your next project.

FAQ

What is an owner-dependent business?

A business whose revenue, operations or customer relationships rely heavily on the owner personally, so that it would struggle to perform if the owner stepped away.

How much does owner dependence reduce business value?

It varies by business. Value Builder System data showed offers averaging 2.93 times pre-tax profit when owners personally knew and served every customer, versus 4.49 times when owners rarely served customers directly. In my experience, a heavily owner-dependent business often sells for something closer to half of a comparable business that runs without its owner, and usually with more of the price tied up in seller financing or earnouts.

Can I still sell a business that depends on me?

Often yes, especially to a buyer with the right skills who plans to run it themselves. Expect a lower multiple, tougher terms and a longer transition period.

How long does it take to reduce owner dependence?

Usually one to three years. Buyers want to see the business performing without you for a meaningful stretch, ideally a full year of financials with a manager in place.

What is the difference between SDE and EBITDA?

SDE adds one owner's salary and benefits back to profit, so it shows what an owner-operator takes home. EBITDA generally treats management as a cost of running the business. Smaller, owner-run businesses are usually priced on SDE, and larger managed businesses on EBITDA.

Will hiring a manager lower my business value?

It lowers your SDE by the manager's pay, but it can raise the multiple buyers will pay and widen the pool of buyers. Whether the trade is worth it depends on your numbers, which is something a valuation can show you.
